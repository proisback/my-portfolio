Stop Trying to Get Hired

Isabella: The traditional path of landing a Product Management role through credentials and resumes is fundamentally broken in a highly competitive tech landscape that increasingly demands immediate proof of functional execution. Many professionals spend months, sometimes years, collecting certifications, attending bootcamps, and optimizing keywords on their LinkedIn profiles, only to find themselves locked out of the industry because they lack the practical evidence of having actually shipped a product. This disconnect creates a massive barrier for mid-career professionals, such as business analysts, who possess deep execution skills but struggle to prove they can decide what to build and why.

Daniel: The shift from being a business analyst to a product manager highlights this tension perfectly. In business analysis, especially within structured corporate environments like banking, insurance, or operations, the role is structured around solving problems that have already been identified. Someone else has defined the business objective, and the analyst translates those goals into functional requirements. The intellectual hunger that drives people toward product management is the desire to move upstream. It is the realization that finding the right question to ask is more valuable than organizing the answers to a question that might not even matter.

Isabella: But moving upstream is where the friction begins. When people try to make this pivot, they often start by seeking out theory. They sign up for cohorts, like the AI-first Product Management programs, to learn the concepts: customer discovery, prioritization matrices, and trade-off analysis. Yet, there is a sharp drop-off when those framework-heavy ideas meet the messy reality of execution. Why does formal training so often fail to prepare candidates for the day-to-day realities of shipping software?

Daniel: Theoretical frameworks offer a sense of order that does not exist in the wild. A prioritization matrix like RICE looks clean on a presentation slide, but it cannot account for shifting market dynamics, sudden technical debt, or human bias during stakeholder discussions. Relying solely on cohorts and courses creates a false sense of security. It equips candidates with the vocabulary of product management, allowing them to speak the language fluently, but it does not develop the muscle memory required to make hard decisions under uncertainty. The only way to bridge that gap is to stop treating product management as a subject to study and start treating it as a craft to practice. That means picking up a problem, choosing a set of trade-offs, and actually building something functional.

Isabella: That argument assumes everyone can simply build. Historically, the barrier to building functional software was incredibly high. If you did not know how to write code, you were relegated to creating static mockups or slide decks. Now, the rise of AI-native development tools has changed that dynamic. We are seeing a major shift where individuals can use natural language to prototype and ship working versions of their ideas. How has this technological shift changed the definition of what a product manager must be?

Daniel: The emergence of what the industry calls vibe coding—using conversational prompts with tools like Cursor, v0, or Loveable to generate production-ready code—means that the technical bottleneck has evaporated. A product manager no longer has to wait for an engineering team to validate a concept. This has led to the rise of the product builder, a hybrid role identified in the 2026 CPO Impact Report. These are individuals who merge product strategy, design thinking, and rapid technical prototyping. When the cost of generating software drops to near zero, the premium shifts entirely to human judgment. The critical question is no longer, can we build this? The question is, should we build this, and does it actually make sense to the person using it?

Isabella: That shift places a huge emphasis on execution details, particularly design and user psychology. When anyone can generate a basic functional interface, the differentiator becomes the quality of the user experience. This is where personal creativity becomes a strategic tool. Some call it an obsession with micro-interactions, copy clarity, and simplified user journeys. But some might argue that getting caught up in making an interface pretty is a distraction from core product strategy. How do you draw the line between useful refinement and mere decoration?

Daniel: The distinction lies in user cognitive load. Beautiful design is not just about aesthetics; it is about reducing the friction between a human brain and a machine. Good copywriting, for example, is one of the most underrated product skills. Clear, concise copy guides a user through a workflow far better than a complex onboarding tutorial. Micro-interactions and motion design are not mere decoration if they provide instant feedback that a system has registered an action. When you focus on simplifying an interface—actively choosing to remove features rather than adding more complexity—you are making a strategic product decision. You are prioritizing user clarity over product bloat. That is where craft meets strategy.

Isabella: This design philosophy can even extend to how professionals present themselves. The standard chronological resume is a highly static, institutional document that rarely captures how a product thinker actually operates. Some professionals are completely reimagining this format. Instead of a standard portfolio, they represent their career journey through metaphorical experiences, like a flight path taking off from one destination and landing in another, using each stop to unpack what they built, what they learned, and how their thinking evolved. Is that level of storytelling genuinely effective, or does it risk looking gimmicky to a traditional hiring manager?

Daniel: It is a deliberate risk, but one that filters for the right kind of attention. A standard resume lists past responsibilities, but a product portfolio structured as a narrative shows product judgment in action. By framing a career transition as a journey with milestones, specific learnings, and strategic course corrections, you are treating your own career as a product. You are showing that you understand user experience, storytelling, and communication. A traditional corporate recruiter might find it unusual, but a product leader looking for creative, self-directed builders will immediately see it as a proof of work that stands out in a sea of identical bullet-point PDFs.

Isabella: Even the most creative portfolio or polished app means very little if it exists in a vacuum. You can design a flawless user interface and write elegant copy, but if nobody actually uses the product, you have no way of validating your design assumptions. This brings us to the hard reality of distribution. Why is building an audience or a distribution channel now an essential part of the modern product builder's toolkit?

Daniel: Because building a product is only half the battle. Without users, you are just performing product management in an echo chamber. You need real people to interact with what you have made, break your assumptions, and ask why you built it a certain way. By actively building in public and sharing insights on networks like LinkedIn with consistent updates, you are not just trying to gather followers. You are building a micro-distribution system. When you ship a new prototype or launch a tool, you have an immediate pool of fellow PMs, founders, and target users who can test it, give you brutal feedback, and force you to iterate. That real-world feedback loop is worth infinitely more than a hypothetical case study in a classroom.

Isabella: Consistency is incredibly difficult to maintain here. Posting five or six times a week while building projects requires a massive commitment. Some critics would say this looks less like product management and more like personal branding or content marketing. How do you ensure that the time spent sharing and networking on social media does not dilute the actual work of building and learning?

Daniel: The key is to treat sharing as a byproduct of the learning process rather than a separate chore. It is a continuous loop: see an interesting technology, experiment with it, build a functional project, learn from the friction points, and then openly share those lessons. You are not manufacturing content; you are documenting your progress. If you discover a new capability in an AI tool, use it to solve a specific problem in your project, and then write about what worked and what failed. This kind of transparency builds genuine trust. It attracts other builders who want to collaborate, and it signals to potential employers that your curiosity is active and practical, not passive and academic.

Isabella: This whole approach fundamentally redefines how we think about career transitions. The old model was highly linear: learn the theory, apply for the job, get the title, and then start doing the work. The new framework is cyclical: learn, build, distribute, collect feedback, iterate, and share. Under this model, the formal job offer and the title of Product Manager are not the starting point. They are the lagging indicators of a transition that has already occurred.

Daniel: Exactly. You do not wake up one day and suddenly become a product manager because someone handed you a business card with a new title. You become a product manager by thinking like one, making the hard trade-offs, getting your hands dirty with technology, and constantly putting your ideas in front of users. You create undeniable proof of your capabilities through action. When you do that consistently, the industry eventually has no choice but to let the title catch up to the work you are already doing.

Isabella: This hands-on methodology challenges the gatekeeping that has defined the tech industry for a generation. If you know someone who is struggling to make a career transition and wants to move from theoretical planning to real execution, share this episode with them.
